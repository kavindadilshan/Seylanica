"Quill" is one of those trade words that sounds more mysterious than it is. A quill is just a long stick of cinnamon. But the way a Ceylon quill is built, from dozens of thin strips rolled together by hand, is the whole reason it looks, snaps and tastes the way it does.

## From shoot to quill

Ceylon cinnamon bushes are cut back hard so they send up straight young shoots. When those shoots are ready, usually after rain when the sap is flowing, they're cut and taken straight to the peeler.

The peeler scrapes off the thin outer bark with a curved knife. Then comes the step most people have never heard of: they rub the stem firmly with a brass rod. That pressure loosens the inner bark from the wood. With a few careful cuts, the inner bark lifts off in long, thin strips.

Now the rolling. The peeler lays strips end to end, overlapping them, and rolls them together into one long quill. Short pieces get tucked into the middle as filler. A skilled peeler can make a quill that looks like a single seamless roll, even though it's built from many pieces.

Fresh quills then dry in the shade for several days. As they lose moisture they curl tighter and turn from pale cream to golden tan. We cover the whole process, and the people behind it, in [the peeler's craft](/blog/cinnamon-peeling-craft).

## Why 42 cm?

It's simply the length the trade settled on over generations. It suits the drying racks, it packs neatly into cartons and bales, and buyers know what to expect. Retail sticks are cut down from these long quills, most often to 5, 8 or 10 cm.

## How quills are graded

Under the Sri Lanka Standard SLS 81, quills are graded mainly by **diameter**. Graders also look at how many whole quills make up a kilo, how much "foxing" (reddish-brown patches) there is on the surface, and how much is broken.

The four families, thinnest to thickest:

| Family | Grades | What they're used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alba | Alba | Luxury retail, gift packs, fine dining |
| Continental | C5 Special, C5, C4, C3 | Gourmet retail, importers, food manufacturers |
| Mexican | M5, M4 | Latin American markets, infusion |
| Hamburg | H1, H2, H3 | Grinding and industrial extraction |

To give you real numbers, our own typical diameters are 6 mm or less for Alba, about 8 to 10 mm for C5 Special, about 10 to 12 mm for C5 and about 13 to 14 mm for C4. The binding figures for any order are on the spec sheet and the lot's lab report. Our [grades guide](/blog/ceylon-cinnamon-grades-guide) goes through each one.

## Thin quills cost more for a reason

A thin, even quill is harder to make. The strips have to be finer, the overlap neater, the handling gentler. Only the most experienced peelers produce Alba consistently, and only part of any harvest makes the top grades. So price tracks skill and scarcity quite directly.

That doesn't mean you should always buy the thinnest quill you can afford. If your cinnamon is going into a grinder, you're paying for appearance you'll never see.

## Whole, cut or broken

- **Whole quills** at 42 cm are the standard bulk format for importers and repackers.
- **Cut sticks** suit retail jars, tubes and cafés. We discuss the options in [cut stick formats](/blog/cut-cinnamon-sticks-sizes).
- **Quillings and featherings**, the broken pieces and shavings left over, go mainly into powder and tea blends. More on those in [quillings, featherings and chips](/blog/quillings-featherings-chips).

## What we'd look at when buying quills

Measure a handful of quills with calipers and check they sit in the right diameter range. Look for even colour and a tight roll. Ask for moisture (we work to 12% or less, tested by ISO 939), because damp quills can go mouldy in a container. Ask for volatile oil too, since that's your best single measure of aroma. And look at how much is broken; a few pieces are normal, a bag full of fragments is not.
