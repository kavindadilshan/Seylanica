**A phytosanitary certificate is an official government document confirming that a plant product has been inspected and found to meet the plant health requirements of the importing country.** For Ceylon cinnamon, it is issued by Sri Lanka's **National Plant Quarantine Service (NPQS)**. Here is how it works and what to check.

## Why plant health certificates exist

Plant products can carry pests and diseases that threaten agriculture in the importing country. Under the International Plant Protection Convention (IPPC), countries set import conditions and rely on the exporting country's national plant protection organisation to certify consignments.

## How it works for cinnamon

1. **Importer confirms requirements.** The destination's import conditions say whether a certificate is needed and whether any additional declarations or treatments are required.
2. **Exporter applies to NPQS** with consignment details.
3. **Inspection.** NPQS officers inspect the consignment, typically by sampling packages for pests, soil and contamination.
4. **Issuance.** If compliant, NPQS issues the certificate, usually in the IPPC model format; electronic certificates (ePhyto) are increasingly used.
5. **Documents travel** with the shipment, or electronically, to the importer.

## What is on the certificate

- Exporter and consignee names and addresses.
- Place of origin.
- Means of conveyance and point of entry.
- Botanical name and description of the product.
- Number and type of packages, quantity.
- Distinguishing marks such as lot numbers.
- Additional declarations, if required.
- Treatment details, if any.
- Issuing officer, date and stamp.

## What importers should check

- **Consignee** matches your import declaration.
- **Description and botanical name** match the invoice: *Cinnamomum verum* or *C. zeylanicum*.
- **Quantities** match the packing list.
- **Additional declarations** required by your country are present.
- **Dates** are consistent with shipment.

Errors can mean holds at the border, so check a draft before the original is issued. See [the export documents checklist](/blog/export-documents-ceylon-cinnamon).

## Does every market require one?

Requirements depend on the destination and the form of the product, and they change. For example, the EU's plant health rules, Great Britain's rules and those of countries such as Australia each set their own conditions. Confirm with your import authority or customs broker before your first shipment. See [importing into the EU](/blog/importing-cinnamon-into-eu) and [Australia, Canada and the Middle East](/blog/importing-cinnamon-australia-canada-middle-east).

## Phyto is not a food safety certificate

A phytosanitary certificate is about **plant pests**, not food safety. It does not cover microbiology, pesticide residues or contaminants. Those are covered by the Certificate of Analysis. See [what a COA should include](/blog/certificate-of-analysis-cinnamon).

## Summary

The phytosanitary certificate is a small document that can stop a shipment if it is wrong. Confirm your market's requirements, check the draft, and keep it with your COA and origin documents.
