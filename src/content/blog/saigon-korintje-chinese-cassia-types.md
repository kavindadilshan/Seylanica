If you sell Ceylon cinnamon, sooner or later a customer will ask how it compares to "Saigon cinnamon" or "Korintje cinnamon". Both are cassia. So is Chinese cinnamon. They're three related but distinct species, and they're worth understanding because they're what Ceylon is competing against on most shelves.

## The four in one table

| Type | Species | Main origin | Flavour | Natural coumarin |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ceylon | *C. verum* | Sri Lanka | Sweet, delicate, floral | Very low |
| Saigon | *C. loureiroi* | Vietnam | Very intense, hot, sweet | High |
| Korintje | *C. burmannii* | Indonesia | Strong, smooth, a little bitter | High |
| Chinese | *C. cassia* | China | Strong, pungent, a little bitter | High |

Coumarin swings a lot within each cassia type, so treat "high" as a direction, not a number. The only figure that matters for an actual shipment is the lab result on its COA. We explain why in [coumarin in cinnamon](/blog/coumarin-in-cinnamon-explained).

## Saigon cassia

Vietnamese cassia has a cult following among some American bakers, and you can see why. It's loud. High in volatile oil, sweet and fiery at the same time, it gives that big red-hot cinnamon hit. The same qualities make it a poor fit if you're after subtlety, or if your product has to stay within coumarin limits.

## Korintje cassia

Named after the Kerinci region of Sumatra, Indonesian cassia is the everyday cinnamon of much of the world. It's affordable, it's consistent, and it's a bit smoother than Chinese cassia. If you've bought a generic jar of ground cinnamon in North America, it was probably Korintje.

## Chinese cassia

Chinese cassia is central to a lot of Chinese cooking, including five-spice. The bark is thick and very hard, and the flavour is pungent with a slightly bitter edge. It's also a major source of cassia oil.

## Where Ceylon stands apart

All three cassias share a basic structure: one thick layer of bark. Ceylon is built differently, from many paper-thin layers rolled together, which makes it brittle and easy to grind. (If you want to check what you have, see [how to tell if your cinnamon is really Ceylon](/blog/how-to-identify-real-ceylon-cinnamon).)

Flavour-wise, Ceylon is the quiet one in the group: sweet, floral, citrus-bright, without the burn. It also has very little natural coumarin, and it comes from a single country with a protected name in the EU.

## Choosing for a product

Here's roughly how we'd think about it:

- **Premium retail, dairy desserts, delicate pastry, tea:** Ceylon. The flavour fits, and the origin story sells.
- **Anything marketed as "true cinnamon" or "Ceylon":** obviously Ceylon, with documents to back it up.
- **Cinnamon-heavy foods for the EU market**, like seasonal bakery or cereals: Ceylon makes coumarin compliance much easier.
- **Mass-market products where punch and lowest cost win:** cassia can work, provided coumarin isn't an issue for you.

One practical note if you're switching from cassia to Ceylon: don't expect a one-for-one swap to taste the same. Ceylon is gentler, so most people end up using somewhat more of it. Our [baking guide](/blog/baking-with-ceylon-cinnamon) has a simple method for finding the right dose.
