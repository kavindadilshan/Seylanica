If we had to pick one number on a cinnamon lab report that most often decides whether a shipment arrives happily or in a claim, it would be moisture. It's simple to measure and expensive to ignore.

## Why it matters so much

Cinnamon is dried bark. Keep it dry and it's very stable. Let moisture creep up and a few things go wrong at once:

- **Mould.** Damp bark sealed in a bag on a warm, weeks-long voyage is exactly what mould likes.
- **You pay for water.** Every extra percent of moisture is weight you're buying at cinnamon prices.
- **Texture problems.** Powder cakes and clumps. Over-dried quills, on the other hand, turn brittle and break.

## The test: ISO 939

ISO 939 is a distillation method. The sample is distilled with a solvent that doesn't mix with water; the water comes over, separates out, and is measured on its own.

The reason it matters is the alternative. A simple oven-drying test drives off everything that evaporates, including some of the volatile oils that give cinnamon its aroma, and counts all of it as water. That overstates moisture in aromatic spices. When you look at a COA, check that moisture was measured by ISO 939 or an equivalent validated method. Our guide to [a good COA](/blog/certificate-of-analysis-cinnamon) covers the other lines.

## What limits look like

| Product | Our specification |
| --- | --- |
| Quills (Alba to C4) | 12% m/m or less |
| Chips | 12% m/m or less |
| Powder | 10% m/m or less |

These line up with common trade expectations. Your own spec might be tighter, depending on your process and how long you hold stock.

## Keeping it dry from garden to warehouse

**At origin**, it starts with slow, thorough shade drying after peeling (we describe the process in [drying and processing](/blog/cinnamon-drying-processing)), testing before packing rather than only after, and dry storage off the floor.

**In packing**, lined cartons or bags, vacuum packing for fine quills, and desiccants where they make sense.

**In the container**, things get interesting. Sea containers heat up during the day and cool at night. Warm, humid air condenses on the cold steel roof and drips onto whatever's below. The trade calls it container rain, and it can ruin perfectly good cargo. The usual defences:

- only load product that's at spec moisture;
- hang container desiccants;
- line the floor and walls with kraft paper;
- avoid long waits in hot ports.

There's more on loading in [how much cinnamon fits in a container](/blog/container-loading-cinnamon).

**At destination**, unload promptly, store cool and dry, and measure moisture yourself on arrival. If it's crept up during the voyage, you want to know before the product goes anywhere.
