Anyone can write "premium quality" on a website. The Certificate of Analysis is where a supplier has to put numbers behind the words. It's the single most useful quality document in the cinnamon trade, and also one of the easiest to get wrong, either by accepting a weak one or by not reading it at all.

## It's about one lot, not the product

A specification describes what a product is supposed to be. A COA reports what one particular lot actually measured. That distinction is everything. The first thing to check on any COA is that it carries a lot number, and that the same number appears on the bags, the packing list and the invoice.

## What should be on it

**Identification.** The lot number, the product and its form and grade (for example "Ceylon cinnamon quills, C5, SLS 81"), the botanical name *Cinnamomum verum*, the country of origin, and the production and sampling dates.

**Who tested it.** The lab's name and address, its accreditation for the relevant tests (ISO/IEC 17025 is the usual standard), a report number, a date and a signature.

**Physical and chemical results**, each next to its limit and the method used:

| Test | Method | Why you care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture | ISO 939 | Mould risk in transit |
| Volatile oil | ISO 6571 | How aromatic the lot is |
| Total ash | ISO 928 | General cleanliness |
| Acid-insoluble ash | ISO 930 | Sand and soil |
| Foreign matter | ISO 927 | Stems, leaves, stones |
| Mesh (powder) | Sieve analysis | Texture |

**Microbiology**, especially for powder: total plate count, yeasts and moulds, *E. coli*, and *Salmonella* absent in 25 g. More in [microbiological testing](/blog/microbiology-testing-spices).

**Contaminants**: pesticide residues against your market's limits, heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic), sulphur dioxide where relevant, and mycotoxins if your QA plan calls for them.

**Coumarin**, reported as a measured value by HPLC. It confirms you're dealing with Ceylon and helps with compliance in regulated foods. See [coumarin in cinnamon](/blog/coumarin-in-cinnamon-explained).

## What a weak COA looks like

We'd be cautious if we saw:

- no lot number, or one that doesn't match the goods;
- results with no methods or limits beside them;
- exactly the same round numbers lot after lot;
- no lab name, date or signature;
- a powder COA with no microbiology or contaminants at all.

None of these prove anything is wrong. They just mean you don't have evidence that it's right.

## How we'd use one

1. **Before ordering**, ask for the COA for the specific lot you're being offered.
2. **Compare** every line against your own specification.
3. **Match the lot number** to the samples you approved, and later to the goods that arrive.
4. **Spot-check** now and then by sending a sample to your own lab.
5. **File it.** You'll need it for audits, customer questions and, if it ever comes to it, a recall.
