When buyers see their first Ceylon quote next to a cassia price list, the reaction is usually somewhere between surprise and suspicion. Several times the price for "cinnamon"? It's a fair question, and the answer is mostly about how the two are made.

## The peeling is done by hand

Every Ceylon quill is made by a person. They scrape the outer bark off a freshly cut shoot, rub it with a brass rod, lift the inner bark off in thin strips and roll those strips into a quill. It takes years to get good at, and even a fast, experienced peeler only produces so much in a day.

Cassia is different. The bark is thick and is stripped off trees in big pieces with far less handling.

The finer the grade, the more this matters. Rolling Alba, at 6 mm or less across, takes the most skill and the most time per kilo. That's why Alba sits at the top of the price list. If you want to see what's involved, read [the peeler's craft](/blog/cinnamon-peeling-craft).

## There isn't much land

Commercial Ceylon cinnamon comes mostly from a strip of Sri Lanka's southern and western wet zone. Land there is shared with other crops, housing and everything else a growing country needs. When world demand rises, supply can't simply double the following year. Cassia, grown at scale across several countries, has much more room to expand. We describe the growing areas in [where Ceylon cinnamon grows](/blog/where-ceylon-cinnamon-grows-sri-lanka).

## Yields are modest

Ceylon cinnamon is harvested from young shoots of low, coppiced bushes, usually every couple of years. The amount of usable inner bark per hectare isn't large. Cassia comes from the thick bark of trees, which gives a lot more bark per tree.

## Fewer people want to peel

This is the pressure that worries the industry most. Peeling is skilled, physical work, and fewer young Sri Lankans are taking it up. Wages have risen as a result, and in tight seasons there simply aren't enough hands. It's a real, long-term factor in price, and we've written about it in [the skills gap](/blog/cinnamon-skills-training-peelers).

## Grading takes time

Quills are sorted by diameter, colour and quality into grades. Only part of each harvest makes the top grades; the rest becomes lower grades, quillings, featherings and chips, each priced to match. All that sorting is labour too.

## Testing and documents aren't free

Export cinnamon is tested for moisture, volatile oil, ash, microbiology, pesticide residues, heavy metals and coumarin, and shipped with phytosanitary and origin certificates. That's a real cost per lot. It's also what makes the product sellable in the EU, UK and US, so we'd count it as money well spent rather than overhead.

## How to get Ceylon without overpaying

This is the part we actually talk to buyers about most.

**Don't pay for looks you won't use.** If your cinnamon is going into a grinder or a kettle, Alba is wasted money. A thicker grade like [C4](/blog/c4-cinnamon-grade-uses) gives you the same species and the same low coumarin for less.

**Look at by-grades for powder and extraction.** Quillings, featherings and chips are genuine Ceylon bark at a fraction of the quill price. They're what most good powders are made from anyway. See [quillings, featherings and chips](/blog/quillings-featherings-chips).

**Buy in sensible volumes.** Fixed costs per order (testing, documents, booking) get spread over more kilos as you go up, and prices usually step down at certain thresholds.

**Plan around the harvest and think about contracts.** Buying at the right time and locking in a base volume smooths out the worst price swings. More on that in [annual contracts vs spot buying](/blog/annual-supply-contracts-cinnamon).

## The honest bottom line

Ceylon cinnamon costs more because it costs more to make. That's not marketing. The good news is that most buyers don't need the most expensive grade to get what they're after.
