Every week someone asks us whether Ceylon cinnamon is "just a better grade" of normal cinnamon. It isn't. Ceylon and cassia come from different trees, grown in different countries, processed in different ways. They're about as similar as lemons and limes: related, interchangeable in a pinch, and obviously different once you put them side by side.

Here are the seven differences we think buyers should actually care about.

## 1. They're different species

Ceylon cinnamon is *Cinnamomum verum* (also listed as *C. zeylanicum*), and the bulk of world supply comes from Sri Lanka.

"Cassia" covers three main species:

- *Cinnamomum cassia*, Chinese cassia
- *Cinnamomum burmannii*, Indonesian or Korintje cassia
- *Cinnamomum loureiroi*, Vietnamese or Saigon cassia

So when a label says Ceylon, that's a claim about botany and origin. It has nothing to do with grade. A thick, coarse Ceylon quill is still Ceylon; a beautifully rolled cassia stick is still cassia. We've written more about [the three cassia types](/blog/saigon-korintje-chinese-cassia-types) if you need to explain them to a customer.

## 2. Structure

This is the one you can check in five seconds. Snap a stick and look at the end.

A Ceylon quill is made from many paper-thin strips of inner bark rolled together, so the cut end looks like layered pastry or a rolled newspaper. Cassia is a single thick sheet of bark that curls in from both edges, leaving a hollow tube or a double scroll.

## 3. Texture

Squeeze a Ceylon quill and it cracks and crumbles. Cassia is woody and hard; you'll need a knife or a good grinder. This matters in factories as much as kitchens. Hard cassia bark wears grinding equipment faster, and Ceylon mills more easily into a fine powder.

## 4. Colour

Ceylon runs from light tan to golden brown. Cassia is darker and redder. In powder form the gap closes, though, so we'd never rely on colour alone to judge what's in a bag.

## 5. Flavour

Put a pinch of each on your tongue and you won't need us to tell you. Cassia is hot, sharp and a little bitter, the "red-hot candy" note many people grew up with. Ceylon is sweeter and more delicate, with floral, citrus and faintly clove-like notes and a warmth that builds slowly instead of biting.

Neither is better across the board. Cassia's punch works in some mass-market bakes. Ceylon wins in custards, pastries, dairy, tea and anything where you want the cinnamon to sit alongside other flavours rather than shout over them. Our [baking guide](/blog/baking-with-ceylon-cinnamon) has tips for switching.

## 6. Coumarin

This is the difference that changes buying decisions in food manufacturing.

Coumarin is a natural aromatic compound found in many plants. Cassia contains a lot of it, and the amount varies widely from lot to lot. Ceylon contains very little. The EU sets maximum coumarin levels for certain foods under Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008. Traditional and seasonal bakery products with cinnamon on the label, for example, are capped at 50 mg/kg, and desserts at just 5 mg/kg.

If you're making a cinnamon-heavy product for the European market, cassia can push you uncomfortably close to those limits. Ceylon gives you room. For any specific shipment, the number that counts is the HPLC result on that lot's Certificate of Analysis. There's more on this in [coumarin in cinnamon](/blog/coumarin-in-cinnamon-explained).

## 7. Price

Ceylon costs several times more than cassia, and there are good reasons for it. Every quill is hand-peeled by a skilled worker. The growing area is small and can't expand quickly. Yields per hectare are lower. Cassia, by comparison, is stripped from trees in thick slabs and grown at scale in several countries.

If the price gap is a problem for your product, don't automatically go back to cassia. A thicker Ceylon grade, or powder milled from quillings, often gets you the flavour and the low coumarin at a much friendlier cost. ## The quick reference

| | Ceylon | Cassia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Species | *C. verum* | *C. cassia*, *C. burmannii*, *C. loureiroi* |
| Main origin | Sri Lanka | China, Indonesia, Vietnam |
| Structure | Many thin rolled layers | One thick curl |
| Texture | Brittle | Hard, woody |
| Flavour | Sweet, floral, gentle | Hot, pungent |
| Natural coumarin | Very low | Much higher |
| Price | Higher | Lower |

## Which one should you buy?

Go with Ceylon if you need a delicate flavour, a credible origin story, low coumarin for regulated foods, or a genuinely premium product. Stick with cassia if raw intensity and the lowest possible price per kilo are what matter, and coumarin isn't a constraint for your product.
