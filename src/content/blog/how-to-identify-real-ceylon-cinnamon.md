Most fake "Ceylon" cinnamon isn't fake in a dramatic way. It's usually cassia sold under a vaguer label, or Ceylon powder quietly cut with cheaper cassia powder. Sticks are easy to check. Powder is harder. Here's how we'd go about it, starting with the five-second test and ending with the lab.

## Start with the cut end

Snap a stick in half, or look closely at the end of one.

Real Ceylon cinnamon is made from many thin strips of inner bark rolled together, so you'll see layer after layer, packed in like a cigar. The centre is often filled with smaller pieces. Cassia is one thick piece of bark that curls in from the sides, so you see a hollow tube or a double scroll with a thick wall.

If you only do one check, do this one. It works on cut sticks as well as long quills, and it's very hard to fake. Our article on [how quills are made](/blog/cinnamon-quills-explained) explains why the structure looks the way it does.

## Then feel it

Press a Ceylon stick between your thumb and finger. It cracks, flakes and crumbles. Cassia barely gives. If a stick sold as Ceylon needs a knife to break, we'd be asking questions.

## Look at the colour, but don't trust it alone

Ceylon is usually light tan to golden. Cassia is darker and redder. That said, drying conditions, grade and age all shift the colour of Ceylon, and older stock darkens. Colour is a supporting clue, nothing more.

## Smell and taste

Rub a little between your fingers. Ceylon smells sweet and floral, with hints of citrus and clove. Cassia is sharper and hotter. Taste them side by side and the difference is obvious, even to people who claim they can't tell spices apart. Our [flavour guide](/blog/ceylon-cinnamon-flavour-profile) gives you words for what you're noticing.

## Check the documents

For trade purchases, authenticity is as much about paperwork as product. A supplier selling genuine Ceylon should be able to give you:

- A **lot number** that appears on the bags, the invoice and the lab report.
- A **Certificate of Analysis** with named test methods and results.
- A **certificate of origin** showing Sri Lanka.
- A **phytosanitary certificate** from Sri Lanka's National Plant Quarantine Service, if your market requires one.

If the lot numbers don't line up across those documents, treat that as a red flag even if the product looks fine. The full list is in our [export documents checklist](/blog/export-documents-ceylon-cinnamon).

## For powder, use a lab

Once bark is ground, the structure is gone, and with it most of your easy checks. This is where labs earn their fee.

The most common test is **coumarin by HPLC**. Cassia naturally contains far more coumarin than Ceylon, so a result that's much higher than expected points to cassia or a blend. Labs can also compare the profile of compounds in the volatile oil, which differs between species. Some offer DNA-based identification, and trained microscopists can recognise the cell structures of different barks.

You don't need to run all of these on every lot. A reasonable approach is to get coumarin on every powder COA and send an occasional sample to an independent lab yourself. We go into more depth in [how cassia blending is detected](/blog/cinnamon-adulteration-detection).

## Warning signs we'd take seriously

- "Ceylon" at a price close to cassia. Hand-peeled cinnamon can't be produced that cheaply.
- Wording like "Ceylon type" or "Ceylon style" instead of a clear origin.
- A supplier who won't send samples from the lot they're offering.
- Documents with no lot number, or lot numbers that don't match.

## The short version

For sticks, trust the cut end and the snap. For powder, trust the lab report and the paperwork, and verify now and then with your own test.
