For most of the spice world, coumarin is just one line on a lab report. For anyone making cinnamon-heavy foods for Europe, it can decide which cinnamon you're allowed to use. It's also one of the clearest ways to tell Ceylon from cassia. So it's worth understanding properly.

## What it is

Coumarin is an aromatic compound found naturally in a lot of plants, including cassia, tonka beans and sweet clover. In cinnamon it isn't added; it's simply there, and how much there is depends mainly on the species.

Cassia (*C. cassia*, *C. burmannii* and *C. loureiroi*) is naturally high in coumarin, and the level varies a lot between lots. Ceylon cinnamon (*C. verum*) is naturally very low. That gap is so consistent that labs use coumarin as one of the main indicators of whether "Ceylon" powder has been blended with cassia, which we cover in [how cassia blending is detected](/blog/cinnamon-adulteration-detection).

## How Europe regulates it

The European Food Safety Authority set a tolerable daily intake for coumarin of 0.1 mg per kg of body weight. Building on that, the EU flavourings regulation, Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008, does two things. It bans adding coumarin to food as a pure substance, and it caps how much coumarin can be present in certain foods when it comes from ingredients like cinnamon.

| Food (EU) | Maximum coumarin |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional and/or seasonal bakery with a reference to cinnamon in the labelling | 50 mg/kg |
| Breakfast cereals, including muesli | 20 mg/kg |
| Fine bakery wares other than traditional/seasonal | 15 mg/kg |
| Desserts | 5 mg/kg |

Always check the current consolidated text and any national guidance, and take proper regulatory advice for your product. Other countries approach this differently.

## Why it pushes manufacturers towards Ceylon

Think of a seasonal spiced biscuit. Lots of cinnamon is the whole point. With cassia, the coumarin from all that cinnamon can bring the finished product close to the limit, and because cassia varies so much, one lot might be fine and the next not. Ceylon's naturally low coumarin gives formulators headroom to use as much cinnamon as the recipe needs. Our article on [Ceylon cinnamon in food manufacturing](/blog/cinnamon-food-manufacturing) goes into that in more detail.

## How it's tested

Labs measure coumarin by **high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)**, usually with UV or mass-spectrometric detection. Results come in mg/kg or as a percentage (1% is 10,000 mg/kg). Ask for the method and the limit of quantification on the COA, and make sure the sample was representative, ideally taken after milling for powder.

## Setting your own limit

Work backwards. Start from your finished product's limit, divide by the maximum amount of cinnamon in your recipe, and leave a margin for natural variation. Then write it into your spec:

> Coumarin: maximum [your limit] mg/kg, determined by HPLC, reported per lot on the Certificate of Analysis.
