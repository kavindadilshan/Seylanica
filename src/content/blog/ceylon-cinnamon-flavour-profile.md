People who grew up on supermarket cinnamon sometimes taste Ceylon for the first time and say it's "weak". Give it a second try in warm milk or a custard and the reaction changes. It isn't weaker; it's different. Cassia hits you straight away. Ceylon unfolds.

This is a guide to that flavour: how to describe it, what changes it, and how to taste it properly when you're choosing a grade.

## The basic profile

| | Ceylon | Typical cassia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aroma | Sweet, floral, citrus peel, honey | Sharp, hot, "red-hot candy" |
| Taste | Gentle sweetness, light wood | Strong, pungent, slightly bitter |
| Heat | Soft warmth | Noticeable burn |
| Finish | Clean, aromatic, lingers | Lingering heat |

The full botanical comparison is in [Ceylon vs cassia](/blog/ceylon-vs-cassia-cinnamon-differences).

## Words that help

When our team talks about a lot, these are the kinds of words we use.

**At the top:** citrus peel, floral, bright, honeyed, fresh.

**In the middle:** warm spice, clove-like, sweet wood.

**Underneath:** soft woodiness, dry bark, a faint earthiness.

And the ones you don't want to hear: **musty** or **damp** (usually a moisture problem), **flat** or **dusty** (old stock, or low volatile oil), **smoky** or **acrid** (something went wrong in processing), and **sulphurous** (heavy fumigation).

## What makes one lot taste different from another

**Volatile oil.** Most of the aroma lives in the essential oil in the bark, measured by ISO 6571. Higher numbers usually mean a more aromatic lot. It's the closest thing to an objective flavour score on a lab report. More in [volatile oil explained](/blog/volatile-oil-cinnamon-iso-6571).

**Grade.** The finer quills, Alba and C5 Special, tend to taste the most delicate and sweet. Thicker grades like C4 are fuller and woodier, which is actually an advantage in long-cooked dishes.

**Freshness.** Aroma fades over months, faster in powder than in sticks, and faster still in heat and light.

**Processing.** Careful shade drying keeps the aroma intact. Rushed or damp drying can leave a musty edge.

**Where and when it grew.** Conditions shift the balance of compounds a little from lot to lot. That's normal for a natural product.

## A tasting you can run in an afternoon

You don't need a sensory lab. You need a kettle, some cups and an honest palate.

1. **Prepare the samples.** Grind a little of each quill grade fresh, or open sealed powders. Code them so tasters don't know which is which.
2. **Smell them dry.** Rub a pinch between your fingers and note your first impressions.
3. **Infuse.** Steep about 1 g in 100 ml of hot (not boiling) water for five minutes. Water shows you the aroma without sugar or fat getting in the way.
4. **Taste in context.** Then try each in something close to your real product: warm milk, a plain sugar syrup, a simple biscuit dough.
5. **Score it.** Rate aroma strength, sweetness, heat, woodiness and any off-notes from 1 to 5.
6. **Check against the lab report.** See whether your favourite also has the higher volatile oil. It often does.

## Describing it to your own customers

Good sensory language sells without promising anything you can't back up. Something like:

- "Sweet, floral true cinnamon, hand-rolled in Sri Lanka."
- "Delicate citrus and honey notes, with a gentle warmth."

Keep well clear of health or benefit claims. They're a regulatory problem and Ceylon doesn't need them. Our [marketing guide](/blog/compliant-marketing-claims-spices) explains where the lines are.
