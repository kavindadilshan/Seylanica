It's easy to forget, handling cartons of quills in a warehouse, that this is one of the oldest traded goods in the world. For a long stretch of history cinnamon was worth more than its weight in a lot of things, and the people who controlled it went to great lengths to keep it that way. A little of that history still shapes how the trade works today.

## A spice with a secret address

Cinnamon turns up in very old texts from the Mediterranean and the Middle East, used in perfume, ritual and cooking. Some of what ancient writers called cinnamon was probably cassia; the two were confused for a very long time. What's clear is that aromatic barks from South and Southeast Asia were reaching distant markets through long chains of middlemen.

Those middlemen had every reason to stay vague about where the bark came from. Mystery kept prices high. Europe ended up with all sorts of colourful stories about cinnamon's origin, none of them involving a tropical island off the tip of India.

## The Portuguese arrive

Things changed in the early 1500s, when Portuguese ships reached Sri Lanka. They understood immediately what the island's cinnamon was worth. Over the following decades they built coastal forts, took cinnamon as tribute and shipped it back to Europe, trying hard to control the flow.

## The Dutch and the first gardens

In the mid-1600s the Dutch East India Company pushed the Portuguese off the coast and ran cinnamon as a strict monopoly, controlling who peeled it and how much reached the market.

Up to this point, cinnamon came from trees growing wild in the forests. In the second half of the 18th century the Dutch began growing it deliberately in gardens. That shift, from gathering to farming, is the root of the industry we know. Coppicing the bushes for repeated harvests, still the standard method, comes out of this period. We describe how it works in [cultivation and coppicing](/blog/cinnamon-cultivation-coppicing).

## British rule

Britain took control of the coast at the end of the 18th century and later the whole island. Cinnamon stayed a monopoly for a while before the trade was opened in the 19th century. By then cassia and cinnamon grown in other colonies were competing hard, and Ceylon lost its near-total grip on the market. What it never lost was its reputation.

## Independence and a modern industry

Ceylon became independent in 1948 and took the name Sri Lanka in 1972. Cinnamon stayed an important export, grown mostly by smallholders in the southern and western wet zone and peeled by skilled workers, many from families who'd done it for generations.

In recent decades the industry has put real effort into formalising quality and protecting its name:

- Grades were set out in the national standard SLS 81, covered in [our SLS 81 explainer](/blog/sls-81-standard-explained).
- The "Pure Ceylon Cinnamon" mark was introduced to brand genuine product.
- "Ceylon Cinnamon" was registered as a Protected Geographical Indication in the European Union in 2022. We explain what that means in [our PGI article](/blog/ceylon-cinnamon-pgi-geographical-indication).
- Testing and food safety systems have caught up with what buyers in the EU, UK and US expect.

## Why any of this matters when you're buying

The long history of cinnamon and cassia being confused isn't just trivia. It's why, today, a serious buyer asks for the botanical name, the origin documents and a lab report instead of taking a label at face value. And it's why Sri Lanka has spent so much effort protecting the word "Ceylon".

The secrecy is gone. These days a modern lot can be traced from the garden it came from to the container it shipped in, which we think is a much better way to protect value than hiding the address.
