If you've only ever bought cinnamon from a supermarket shelf, there's a good chance you've never tasted Ceylon cinnamon. Most of what's sold as "cinnamon" around the world is cassia, a cousin from a different tree. Ceylon cinnamon is the inner bark of *Cinnamomum verum*, and nearly all of it comes from one island: Sri Lanka.

We spend a lot of our time explaining this to new buyers, so this is the introduction we wish everyone had before their first inquiry.

## The plant behind it

*Cinnamomum verum* is an evergreen in the laurel family. You'll also see it called *Cinnamomum zeylanicum*, an older name taken from "Zeylan", one of the historical names for Sri Lanka. Both names describe the same plant, and we explain the history in [our note on botanical names](/blog/cinnamomum-verum-vs-zeylanicum-names).

Left alone, it grows into a decent-sized tree. Growers don't let it. They cut it back to a low stump every couple of years so it sends up a cluster of straight young shoots, and those shoots are what get harvested. This practice is called coppicing, and it's the reason cinnamon gardens in the south of Sri Lanka look more like dense shrubbery than an orchard.

The spice is the thin layer of bark just under the rough outer skin. That inner bark is where the aroma lives.

## How a quill gets made

This is the part that surprises people. Ceylon cinnamon is still made almost entirely by hand.

A peeler takes a freshly cut shoot, ideally soon after rain when the sap is up, and scrapes off the outer bark. Then they rub the stem firmly with a brass rod. That loosens the inner bark so it can be lifted off in long, thin strips without tearing. The strips are laid end to end, overlapped, and rolled together into a single long quill, traditionally about 42 cm. Small offcuts get packed into the middle. The quills then dry slowly in the shade for several days, curling tighter and turning that familiar golden tan.

Look at the end of a finished quill and you'll see dozens of paper-thin layers, rolled like a cigar. That's the signature of Ceylon cinnamon, and it's the quickest way to tell it apart from cassia. We've written a full piece on [the peeler's craft](/blog/cinnamon-peeling-craft) if you want the detail.

## So how is it different from cassia?

Cassia comes mostly from China, Indonesia and Vietnam, from *Cinnamomum cassia*, *C. burmannii* and *C. loureiroi*. Its bark is stripped off in one thick piece that curls into a hard, hollow tube. You'd struggle to snap it with your fingers.

The flavour is different too. Cassia is hot and punchy. Ceylon is sweeter and softer, with floral and citrus notes and a gentle warmth that doesn't burn. Neither is "wrong", but they behave very differently in food.

There's also a regulatory angle. Cassia naturally contains far more coumarin, a flavouring compound that the EU limits in certain foods. Food manufacturers who need to stay well within those limits often specify Ceylon for that reason alone. Our [Ceylon vs cassia comparison](/blog/ceylon-vs-cassia-cinnamon-differences) goes through the differences one by one.

## How it's graded and traded

In the trade, whole quills are graded mainly by diameter under Sri Lanka Standard SLS 81. Thinner is finer and more expensive.

- **Alba** is the thinnest, at 6 mm or less. It's the luxury grade.
- **Continental grades** (C5 Special, C5, C4 and so on) are the backbone of the export trade.
- **Mexican and Hamburg grades** are progressively thicker and mostly used for grinding and infusion.

Anything that breaks during peeling, rolling or handling doesn't go to waste. Broken quills become quillings, thin shavings become featherings, and trimmings become chips. Most of that ends up as powder, tea-cut or distilled oil.

If grades are new to you, start with our [grades guide](/blog/ceylon-cinnamon-grades-guide).

## What we'd check before buying

Cinnamon is a natural product and every lot is a little different. Whoever you buy from, we'd suggest asking for four things before you commit money:

1. **The grade and the standard behind it.** "Premium" means nothing; "C5, SLS 81" means something you can measure.
2. **A Certificate of Analysis for the actual lot.** At minimum, moisture, volatile oil, ash and, for powder, microbiology. Pesticide residues, heavy metals and coumarin too if you're selling into the EU, UK or US.
3. **Samples from that same lot**, with the lot number on the bag.
4. **The export documents your market expects**, typically a phytosanitary certificate and a certificate of origin.

A supplier who's comfortable with all four is usually a supplier worth talking to. If someone gets vague when you ask for lot data, that tells you something too.
