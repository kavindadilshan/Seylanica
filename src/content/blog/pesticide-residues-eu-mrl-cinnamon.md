Pesticide residues are one of those topics where the importer carries the risk. If a lot turns up over the limit in your market, it's your shipment that gets stopped and your product that gets withdrawn. So it's worth understanding how the limits work and what good testing looks like.

## How the EU does it

The EU sets maximum residue levels (MRLs) under Regulation (EC) No 396/2005, pesticide by pesticide and commodity by commodity. Spices, cinnamon included, have their own entries.

The rule that catches people out is the default: where no specific MRL has been set for a pesticide on a commodity, a limit of **0.01 mg/kg** generally applies. In practice that means "not detectable with routine methods". MRLs also change frequently, so the EU Pesticides Database is the place to check current values.

## The UK

Since Brexit, Great Britain keeps its own MRL schedule. Many limits still match the EU's, but they can and do diverge. Check the GB register for your products. We cover the wider UK picture in [importing into the UK](/blog/importing-cinnamon-into-uk).

## The US

In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency sets **tolerances**. If a residue shows up for which no tolerance exists on that commodity, the food can be treated as adulterated. FDA enforces this at the border. More in [importing into the USA](/blog/importing-cinnamon-into-usa-fda).

## What good testing looks like

- An **ISO/IEC 17025 accredited lab** with spices in its scope.
- A **multi-residue method** by GC-MS/MS and LC-MS/MS that covers several hundred compounds.
- **Single-residue methods** for things the multi-method doesn't catch, if they're relevant to you (dithiocarbamates, glyphosate and chlorates come up).
- A **limit of quantification** at or below 0.01 mg/kg for most compounds, so you can actually check against the EU default.
- **Results per lot**, with the lot number and date.

## Contract wording we'd suggest

> The product shall comply with the maximum residue levels in force in [destination market] at the date of shipment. Each lot shall be accompanied by a multi-residue analysis from an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory showing all results below the applicable MRL.

It's simple, and it puts the obligation in the right place.

## Reducing the risk upstream

Testing catches problems. Sourcing prevents them. Buy from suppliers who know where their bark comes from and assign lot numbers at intake (we explain how in [garden-to-port traceability](/blog/from-garden-to-port-traceability)). Ask about Good Agricultural Practices and what inputs growers use. Re-test the occasional lot yourself on arrival, and keep the records.

## Organic still needs testing

Organic certification restricts synthetic pesticides, but drift from neighbouring fields and contamination in handling can still happen. Organic lots should be tested just like conventional ones. See [organic Ceylon cinnamon](/blog/organic-ceylon-cinnamon).
