**The three main ways to reduce microbes in spices are steam sterilisation, irradiation and fumigation with ethylene oxide (EtO), and they are treated very differently by regulators and consumers.** For export cinnamon, steam sterilisation is generally the most widely accepted choice. Here is how they compare.

## Why treat spices at all

Spices can carry bacteria, including pathogens such as *Salmonella*, which survive drying. When powder goes into ready-to-eat products without a cooking step, buyers often require a treatment to reduce microbial load. See [microbiological testing for cinnamon](/blog/microbiology-testing-spices).

## The options compared

| | Steam sterilisation | Irradiation | Ethylene oxide (EtO) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How it works | Saturated steam, then drying | Ionising radiation (gamma, electron beam, X-ray) | Chemical gas fumigation |
| Residues | None | None | Possible EtO and 2-chloroethanol residues |
| EU status | Accepted | Permitted for dried herbs and spices; must be labelled | Not permitted |
| US status | Accepted | Permitted for spices; must be labelled | Registered by EPA for spices, with tolerances |
| Consumer perception | Positive or neutral | Mixed; labelling required | Negative in many markets |
| Flavour impact | Small, controllable | Minimal | Minimal |

## Steam sterilisation

Steam treatment exposes the spice to saturated steam for a controlled time and temperature, followed by rapid drying. Benefits:

- **No residues** and no special labelling.
- **Accepted in all major markets.**
- **Significant microbial reduction** when validated.

Trade-offs: some loss of volatile aroma if poorly controlled, and the need to dry the product again. Ask for volatile oil before and after treatment. See [volatile oil explained](/blog/volatile-oil-cinnamon-iso-6571).

## Irradiation

Irradiation is effective and leaves no residues, and it is permitted for spices in both the EU and US. But it must be **declared on the label**, and many brands and retailers prefer to avoid it for perception reasons. Organic rules also prohibit it.

## Ethylene oxide

EtO has been used to fumigate spices in some markets. It is **not authorised for food in the EU**, and since 2020 EtO residues found in ingredients such as sesame seeds have led to extensive recalls across Europe. EU buyers now commonly require EtO testing and "no EtO treatment" declarations.

## What to specify

- Treatment method required, or "untreated" if acceptable for your use.
- "No irradiation" and "no ethylene oxide" declarations where needed.
- Microbiological limits after treatment.
- Volatile oil after treatment.
