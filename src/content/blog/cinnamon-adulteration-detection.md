**The main fraud risk in cinnamon is economic substitution: selling cheaper cassia, or a blend of cassia and Ceylon, as pure Ceylon cinnamon.** Because Ceylon costs several times more than cassia, the incentive is real, and ground powder hides it. Here is what to watch for and how labs detect it.

## Common types of cinnamon adulteration

| Type | What happens | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Species substitution | Cassia sold as Ceylon | Price difference |
| Blending | Ceylon powder cut with cassia powder | Price difference, harder to detect |
| Exhausted bark | Bark after oil extraction ground into powder | Low-cost raw material, weak aroma |
| Fillers | Starches, husks or other plant material | Bulk up weight |
| Colourants | Undeclared colours to improve appearance | Mask poor quality |

## How labs detect it

### Coumarin analysis

Cassia contains far more coumarin than Ceylon. An unexpectedly high coumarin result by HPLC in "Ceylon" powder suggests cassia is present. See [coumarin in cinnamon](/blog/coumarin-in-cinnamon-explained).

### Marker compound profiles

The balance of compounds in cinnamon's volatile oil differs by species. Chromatographic profiles can distinguish Ceylon from cassia and help detect blends.

### Low volatile oil

Exhausted bark has had its oil removed. A volatile oil result far below expectations is a warning. See [volatile oil explained](/blog/volatile-oil-cinnamon-iso-6571).

### DNA-based methods

Some laboratories offer DNA identification of plant species in spices, useful for confirming species and detecting foreign plant material.

### Microscopy

Trained analysts can identify characteristic cell structures of Ceylon and cassia bark, and spot fillers.

### Ash and colour tests

High acid-insoluble ash or unusual colour may point to fillers or colourants. See [ash in cinnamon](/blog/cinnamon-ash-acid-insoluble-ash).

## Prevention for buyers

1. **Buy whole quills where possible** and grind locally, or buy from suppliers who mill from identified quills.
2. **Know the supply chain.** Prefer suppliers who trace lots from growers to export. See [garden-to-port traceability](/blog/from-garden-to-port-traceability).
3. **Require lot-specific COAs** including coumarin.
4. **Run independent spot checks** on arrival.
5. **Be wary of prices** that look too good for Ceylon. See [why Ceylon costs more](/blog/why-ceylon-cinnamon-costs-more).
6. **Assess food fraud vulnerability** in your QA system, as many food safety standards now require.

## Summary

Cinnamon fraud thrives where powder, price pressure and opaque supply chains meet. Lab tests can catch it, but traceability prevents it.
