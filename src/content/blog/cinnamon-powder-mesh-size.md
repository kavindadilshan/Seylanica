"Fine powder" means different things to different people. To a home cook it means it doesn't feel gritty. To a factory it might mean it passes through a particular sieve on a particular line without clogging the doser. Mesh size is how the spice trade takes the guesswork out.

## What mesh actually means

Mesh is the number of openings per linear inch in a sieve. More openings mean smaller holes, so **a higher mesh number means a finer powder**.

| US mesh | Opening (approx.) | How it feels | Where it's used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 40 | 420 µm | A little gritty | Rubs, coarse blends, some bakery |
| 60 | 250 µm | Fine | General baking, retail jars, seasonings |
| 80 | 180 µm | Very fine | Drinks, dairy, icings, smooth fillings |
| 100 | 150 µm | Ultra fine | Specialist uses |

Our standard Ceylon powder comes at 60 or 80 mesh, and we can grind to other specs on request. The details are on our [powder spec sheet](/products?spec=powder).

## How fineness changes things

**It spreads more evenly.** Finer powder disperses better through liquids and batters, so you get fewer specks and more consistent flavour.

**It releases aroma faster.** More surface area means the flavour comes out quickly. Lovely in a latte, less ideal if the powder will sit on a shelf for a year.

**Texture.** A 40 mesh grind can feel sandy in a custard. An 80 mesh grind disappears.

**Flow and caking.** Very fine powders clump more easily, especially if moisture creeps up.

**Shelf life.** All that surface area also means aroma escapes faster in storage. Good packaging matters more the finer you go. We cover that in [shelf life and storage](/blog/cinnamon-shelf-life-storage).

## Write it the right way

"60 mesh" on its own is ambiguous. Does that mean every particle passes through? Most of them? We'd always write it as a pass-through percentage:

> Fineness: minimum 95% passing through a 60 mesh (250 µm) sieve.

If dust is a concern on your line, you can add a limit on the finest fraction as well. Then ask for the sieve analysis on the lab report.

## Mesh isn't the whole story

A perfectly milled powder made from poor raw material is still poor powder. Alongside mesh, we'd specify:

- **What it's milled from:** quills, quillings or featherings. See [quillings, featherings and chips](/blog/quillings-featherings-chips).
- **Moisture**, 10% or less in our spec (ISO 939).
- **Volatile oil**, 0.7% v/w or more typical (ISO 6571).
- **Total ash** and **acid-insoluble ash**, 5% and 1% or less in our spec.
- **Microbiology**: TPC, yeasts and moulds, *E. coli* and *Salmonella*. See [microbiological testing](/blog/microbiology-testing-spices).
- **Treatment**: steam-sterilised or untreated.
- **Coumarin**, if your product has limits.

## A word on heat

Grinding creates heat, and heat drives off volatile oils. Good milling keeps temperatures under control so the powder keeps its aroma. It's a fair question to ask any supplier how they mill and whether they test after milling rather than before.

The easiest way to choose is to try both. [Ask for samples](/quote?type=sample) of 60 and 80 mesh and use them in your actual product for a week.
