A spec sheet can look like a wall of numbers. Read it in the right order, though, and it tells you a story: what the product is, how it's graded, how clean and safe it is, and how it'll turn up at your door. Here's the order we'd read one in, and what we'd look for in each part.

## 1. Is it what it says it is?

Start with identity. You want to see:

- The product and its form: quills, cut sticks, chips or powder.
- The botanical name: *Cinnamomum verum* J.Presl (syn. *C. zeylanicum*). If it says *C. cassia* or *C. burmannii*, you're looking at cassia. Our note on [botanical names](/blog/cinnamomum-verum-vs-zeylanicum-names) explains the variations.
- Country of origin: Sri Lanka.
- A product code you can match to quotes and invoices.

## 2. Grade and physical properties

- **Grade with its standard**, such as "C5, SLS 81:2021". "Premium" is not a grade. See [SLS 81 explained](/blog/sls-81-standard-explained).
- **Diameter and length** for quills.
- **Mesh** for powder, ideally as a pass-through percentage.
- **Foreign matter** (ISO 927): stems, leaves, stones.
- Colour and appearance.

## 3. The chemistry

This is where the most useful numbers live.

| Line | What it tells you | Usual method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture | Will it stay sound, or go mouldy? | ISO 939 |
| Volatile oil | How aromatic is it? | ISO 6571 |
| Total ash | Mineral content and general cleanliness | ISO 928 |
| Acid-insoluble ash | How much sand and soil? | ISO 930 |
| Coumarin | Species check and regulatory compliance | HPLC |

We've written separate pieces on [moisture](/blog/cinnamon-moisture-content-iso-939), [volatile oil](/blog/volatile-oil-cinnamon-iso-6571) and [ash](/blog/cinnamon-ash-acid-insoluble-ash) if you want to go deeper.

## 4. Microbiology

Mostly relevant for powder. Look for total plate count, yeasts and moulds, *E. coli*, and *Salmonella*, which should be "absent in 25 g". Also check whether the powder is steam-sterilised or untreated, because that changes what it's suitable for. More in [microbiological testing](/blog/microbiology-testing-spices).

## 5. Contaminants

Pesticide residues against your market's limits, heavy metals (lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic), and sulphur dioxide if it matters for your market. Many sheets will say "per lot", which simply means the result is reported on each lot's COA rather than promised in general.

## 6. Compliance statements

Phytosanitary certification, certificates of origin, and any GI or organic status. Be wary of logos without certificate numbers. A good supplier only states certifications they actually hold.

## 7. The commercial bits

MOQ, lead time, packaging (cartons, bags, liners, pallets), Incoterms offered, shelf life and storage conditions, and whether private label is available. Boring, but this is where a lot of misunderstandings start.

## Things that would make us cautious

- Numbers without a test method.
- Only "typical" values, with no actual limits.
- No botanical name anywhere.
- Certification logos with no certificate details.

## Then check the lot

A spec sheet is half the picture. When an actual lot is offered, compare its COA against the sheet line by line. That's how you catch the occasional lot that drifts.
