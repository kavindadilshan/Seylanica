"Cinnamon oil" sounds like one product. It's really two, and mixing them up is an expensive mistake. Bark oil smells like cinnamon. Leaf oil smells more like cloves. They're priced very differently, used for different things, and both need careful handling.

## Where each comes from

Both are made by steam distillation of *Cinnamomum verum*.

**Bark oil** is distilled from bark, usually chips, featherings and other pieces that aren't good enough for quills. **Leaf oil** comes from the leaves and twigs left over after harvest.

That's part of why the cinnamon industry wastes so little. The quills go to market, the broken bark goes to powder or the bark still, and the leaves go to the leaf still. We wrote about that in [cinnamon by-products](/blog/cinnamon-by-products-zero-waste).

## What's in them

| | Bark oil | Leaf oil |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main component | Cinnamaldehyde | Eugenol |
| Smell | Warm, sweet, unmistakably cinnamon | Spicy, clove-like, woody |
| Colour | Yellow to reddish brown | Yellow to brown |
| Price | High | Much lower |

The exact make-up varies with the raw material, the region and how it was distilled. Don't rely on generic figures; ask for the gas chromatography (GC) profile for the lot you're buying.

## Why bark oil is so much dearer

Bark doesn't yield much oil, and the bark itself is valuable. Leaves are plentiful and give more oil per kilo of input. Put those two facts together and the price gap makes sense.

## What they're used for

**Bark oil** goes into flavourings for drinks, sweets and bakery, into fine fragrance at low levels, and into premium aromatic products.

**Leaf oil** is used where a warm, clove-like note works: fragrance, candles, household products, and as a natural source of eugenol. For flavour, see [infusions, syrups and spirits](/blog/infusing-cinnamon-syrups-spirits).

## Handle them with respect

Both oils are concentrated and both can irritate or sensitise skin.

- Cinnamaldehyde and eugenol are known skin sensitisers. The fragrance industry body IFRA sets limits on how much can be used in different product types.
- In the EU, fragrance allergens including cinnamal (cinnamaldehyde), cinnamyl alcohol and eugenol have to be named on cosmetic labels above certain concentrations.
- Food use is governed by each market's flavouring rules and dosage limits.
- Essential oils can count as dangerous goods for transport. Get the Safety Data Sheet and the transport classification before you book shipping.

## What to ask a supplier for

At minimum: the botanical source (*C. verum*) and which part of the plant, a GC profile showing the main components, basic physical data (relative density, refractive index, optical rotation), the SDS and allergen statement, and a certificate of origin.

## Which one do you need?

Simple test: do you want it to smell like cinnamon? Then you need bark oil, and you'll pay for it. If a warm, clove-like spice note is what you're after, leaf oil does the job at a fraction of the price. Either way, specify by plant part and main component, not just "cinnamon oil".
